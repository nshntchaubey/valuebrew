# ValueBrew — Behavioral Hypothesis Model
### Canonical reference document — built entirely from secondary research already completed. No primary research included or assumed.

**Labeling key used throughout:**
- **Evidence** — directly sourced from a real, cited finding.
- **Inference** — a reasoned conclusion drawn logically from Evidence, not itself independently sourced.
- **Hypothesis** — a testable claim, genuinely uncertain, that could go either way.
- **Assumption** — something taken as given to make the model usable, with no evidence either for or against it.

---

## 1. Behavioral Model

### Decision Pathways
Three structurally distinct pathways, not one process with variations — this split is **Evidence**-based, drawn from independent findings that don't merge cleanly (a habitual cluster that seeks no information sitting alongside a literature-defined impulse mode built on low cognitive control).

**Habitual/Anchored:** trigger → anchor retrieval → availability check → selection. The first three steps are **Evidence**-supported. What happens when the anchor is unavailable is a **Hypothesis** — three candidate resolutions (falls into Deliberate, falls into Impulse, or purchase abandoned) were named, none confirmed.

**Impulse:** trigger/cue → urge → selection, explicitly resistant to further staged decomposition — **Evidence**, since the defining literature describes this mode by the *absence* of multi-stage evaluation, not its presence. Which specific attribute (if any) drives selection inside this mode is unresolved — **Hypothesis**.

**Deliberate/Exploratory:** trigger → framing → input gathering → narrowing → final selection. Framing and input gathering are **Evidence**-supported (occasion-fit literature; the twenty Phase 1 factors). Narrowing and final selection are almost entirely **Hypothesis** — no source describes sequence, elimination order, or tie-break logic.

**Proxy-buying modifier:** attaches most plausibly to Deliberate — **Inference**, since Habitual and Impulse both seem to presuppose the buyer is the consumer. Whether it can attach to the other two pathways is unaddressed — **Assumption**, currently defaulted to "no."

### Decision Timing
Most existing evidence describes the *shelf* moment specifically. A live, unresolved challenge (not yet evidenced either way): whether a meaningful share of decisions actually crystallize earlier — at home, via group coordination, or from prior marketing exposure — with the shelf moment reduced to execution rather than deciding. This is a **Hypothesis**, explicitly flagged as unresolved by any evidence gathered so far, not resolved by anything in this model.

### Decision Questions
Recurring across pathways: "what do I usually get" (Habitual, **Evidence**), "will I like this / can I afford this / is this a trusted brand" (Deliberate input-gathering, **Evidence**), "how do I narrow this down" and "why this one" (**Hypothesis**, unanswered by any source). Structurally absent from every source reviewed: "which option gives me the most value for my money" as a spontaneously-held question — this is the single most important negative finding in the whole model, labeled **Inference** (an absence noted across many independent sources, not a directly stated fact).

### Known Influences
Taste, brand familiarity, price-as-constraint, ABV, packaging format, occasion-fit, novelty, and country of origin are all **Evidence**-supported as real factors, from independent academic sources. Price-as-quality-signal (willingness to pay more, associating price with brand quality) is **Evidence** from a single non-India study — real, but not yet generalized. Social/peer influence and in-store environmental cues are **Hypothesis**-level — plausible, adjacent evidence exists, but nothing directly measures either for off-trade, counter-service retail specifically.

### Known Unknowns
The narrowing mechanism. The final tie-break logic. The habitual-anchor-unavailable fallback. Whether "value for money" is a real latent frame or one that would have to be taught. Where in the journey the real decision crystallizes. Whether individual buyers personally track legal-price fairness, as opposed to industry and advocacy groups, who are the only voices found on this topic anywhere in the research.

## 2. Customer Segments — behavioral, not demographic

**Habitual/Anchored buyers.** Defining characteristic: retrieves a fixed choice from memory, seeks no information, generates no word-of-mouth. Purchase style: fast, low-friction, near-automatic. Confidence: Moderate. Evidence: a real empirical cluster identified in a Brazilian beer-segmentation study matching this exact description. Assumption: that this cluster's size and behavior transfers to Bangalore.

**Deliberate/Exploratory buyers.** Defining characteristic: actively weighs multiple attributes before choosing, engages with occasion-fit. Purchase style: slower, multi-input. Confidence: Moderate. Evidence: occasion-based segmentation research applied specifically to beer, plus existing India-specific occasion content already published by a named competitor. Assumption: that this is a stable trait rather than a situational mode any buyer can enter.

**Impulse-prone buyers/moments.** Defining characteristic: purchase driven by a sudden urge with low cognitive control. Purchase style: fast, but for a different reason than the Habitual segment — absence of deliberation, not presence of a strong default. Confidence: Low-moderate. Evidence: general retail impulse-buying literature (an estimated 30–50%+ of purchases in susceptible categories), not beer- or India-specific. Assumption: that this generalizes to off-trade alcohol retail in Karnataka at all.

**Price-as-signal buyers, distinct from price-as-constraint buyers.** Defining characteristic: treats a higher price as evidence of quality and is willing to pay more for a trusted brand, versus a separate group for whom price is a hard ceiling regardless of quality. Confidence: Low-moderate — single non-India study. Evidence: the Spanish beer-attribute study's brand/price correlation finding. Assumption: this fork exists in the same shape in Karnataka.

**Proxy buyers** (purchasing for someone else's consumption). Defining characteristic: substitutes packaging/brand cues for personal taste knowledge, since taste can't be personally verified. Confidence: Low — single, thin source. Evidence: the label-design study's direct finding that packaging matters more to non-consuming buyers. Assumption: prevalence and frequency in this specific market, entirely unknown.

**Important note applying to all five:** these are best understood as situational modes a single person moves between, not fixed types — Habitual on a routine Tuesday, Deliberate before hosting a weekend gathering. Treating them as personas would misrepresent the evidence. This framing is **Inference**, following directly from earlier findings in this same research process.

## 3. Candidate JTBDs — derived only from the Behavioral Model above, not prioritized

**JTBD-1.** When I need beer and already know what I want, I want to get my usual choice with minimal thought, so I don't have to deliberate over something routine. Trigger: routine need. Current solution: habit and memory; no tool currently used. Confidence: Moderate. Evidence: the Habitual cluster finding. Assumption: this JTBD has no unmet friction at all — see Section 4.

**JTBD-2.** When I have a specific occasion in mind, I want to pick something that fits it, so my choice matches the moment. Trigger: an upcoming or present social occasion. Current solution: partially served already — occasion-based content exists in the market (magicpin). Confidence: Moderate.

**JTBD-3 — the core original bet.** When I'm choosing without a strong existing preference, I want to know which option gives me more for my money, so I don't feel like I overpaid or settled for less than I could have gotten. Trigger: a no-anchor situation. Current solution: none evidenced anywhere. Confidence: **Low** — this is the JTBD with the least direct supporting evidence in the entire model; its underlying frame was never observed occurring naturally in any source reviewed. Flagged explicitly, not softened.

**JTBD-4.** When my usual choice isn't available, I want a fast way to land on something acceptable, so I don't waste time or leave empty-handed. Trigger: anchor interruption. Current solution: unknown. Confidence: Low — the pathway's existence is inferred, its resolution entirely unobserved.

**JTBD-5.** When buying for someone else, I want to pick something they'd likely be glad to receive, so I don't get it wrong on their behalf. Trigger: proxy-purchase situation. Current solution: packaging/brand substitution, per the one source found. Confidence: Low.

**JTBD-6.** When paying, I want confidence that I'm not being charged more than the legal price, so I don't feel cheated. Trigger: the transaction moment. Current solution: government enforcement exists at a systemic level; no individual-level tool found. Confidence: Moderate that the underlying problem is real; **Low** that individuals, rather than only industry and advocacy groups, hold this as a live personal concern.

**JTBD-7.** When an urge hits, I want to satisfy it immediately without deliberation. Trigger: spontaneous cue. Current solution: none needed, by definition. Confidence: Low-moderate, general literature only.

## 4. Candidate Frictions — by JTBD, split into four types

**JTBD-1 (Habitual):** No Information, Decision, or Emotional friction identified — Evidence points toward this JTBD being already well-served. Environmental: availability of the anchor item (Inference — a necessary condition for the pathway to complete, though not itself directly observed).

**JTBD-2 (Occasion-fit):** Information: matching beer attributes to occasion type is not fully solved by anything found (Hypothesis). Decision: weighing multiple attributes at once against an occasion (Evidence, from occasion-segmentation literature). Environmental: counter-service retail format may restrict the browsing needed to explore occasion-fit options (Inference, from the earlier-established structural difference between counter-service and self-browse formats). Emotional: mild social-performance concern when hosting (Hypothesis, weakly evidenced).

**JTBD-3 (Value evaluation):** Information: no source or label anywhere combines price and ABV into one comparable figure — this friction is real and structural (Evidence of absence). Decision: comparing multiple attributes simultaneously under time pressure (Inference, general behavioral-economics literature, not beer-specific). Environmental: counter-service format constrains direct comparison (Inference). Emotional: regret-avoidance ("did I overpay") — Hypothesis, resting mainly on the founder's own single account plus general loss-aversion literature, not independently confirmed for this population.

**JTBD-4 (Fallback):** Information: no evidence on what substitute logic, if any, gets applied (Assumption). Decision: elimination process entirely unobserved (Assumption). Environmental: whether staff proactively suggest alternatives (Assumption). Emotional: plausible frustration at an interrupted routine (Hypothesis, reasonable but unconfirmed).

**JTBD-5 (Proxy buying):** Information: real, evidenced gap — the buyer cannot access the consumer's actual taste preference (Evidence). Decision: substituting packaging/brand signals for missing taste knowledge (Evidence). Emotional: risk of getting it wrong for someone else (Hypothesis).

**JTBD-6 (Price fairness):** Information: buyers likely don't know the current legal MRP at the point of purchase (Assumption — genuinely unknown in either direction). Environmental: real, documented pattern of shops charging above MRP (Evidence). Emotional: anger when overcharging is discovered — weak Evidence, inferred from the tone of persistent public complaints referenced in enforcement reporting, though those complaints came from officials and advocacy voices, not directly from individual buyers.

**JTBD-7 (Impulse):** No Information or Decision friction, by definition. Emotional: the urge itself is the entire mechanism (Evidence, from the definitional literature).

## 5. Opportunity Space — capabilities only, no features

**For JTBD-2 and JTBD-3 (information frictions):** the capability of combining currently-separate facts (price, ABV, format) into one comparable signal. Improve comparison — not display a comparison.

**For JTBD-3 (environmental friction):** the capability of enabling evaluation independent of physical shelf access, given the counter-service constraint. Reduce dependence on browsing.

**For JTBD-4 (fallback):** the capability of surfacing a fast, acceptable substitute the moment a default is unavailable. Reduce decision complexity under interruption.

**For JTBD-5 (proxy buying):** the capability of translating a third party's likely preference into something actionable without requiring real-time communication with them. Increase confidence in decisions made on someone else's behalf.

**For JTBD-6 (price fairness):** the capability of making the legally correct price checkable at the moment of transaction. Increase verifiability.

**For JTBD-1 and JTBD-7 (Habitual, Impulse):** worth stating plainly rather than manufacturing an opportunity that isn't there — the evidence doesn't support meaningful friction in either JTBD, and therefore doesn't support an opportunity area for either one. Not every JTBD needs to resolve into something buildable.

## 6. Confidence Map

| Conclusion | Evidence | Confidence | Largest Remaining Assumption |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three distinct decision pathways exist | Independent sources describing habitual, impulse, and deliberate modes without merging | Moderate-High | That these three fully partition real behavior, with nothing left over |
| Narrowing/final-selection logic within Deliberate pathway | None directly | Low | That it's even a describable, stable sequence rather than context-dependent every time |
| "Value for money" as a natural, spontaneous frame | Absent across all sources reviewed | Low | That its absence in existing literature means absence in real cognition, not just absence in what's been studied |
| Karnataka retailer-level price competition (same SKU, different store) | Direct regulatory/enforcement evidence against it | Low (for the original premise) | That informal/cash-based variation doesn't exist off the record |
| Cross-brand value math is real and current | AIB reform directly ties MRP to alcohol content | Moderate-High | That people would want this calculation done for them rather than not caring |
| Illegal overcharging is a real, recurring pattern | Multi-year enforcement and ministerial statements | High (for the pattern) | That individual buyers personally notice or care, versus only officials and advocacy groups |
| Proxy-buying is a real behavioral mode | Single label-design study | Low | Prevalence in this market, entirely unknown |
| Beer-versus-spirits category risk | Multiple sources agree spirits dominate revenue; volume comparison unresolved | Moderate | Whether beer buyers are numerous/distinct enough regardless of relative category size |
| Decision may crystallize before reaching the shelf | No direct evidence either way; only structural plausibility (group-purchase case, occasion-planning literature) | Low | Entirely open — this is a gap, not a lean |
| Impulse buying is a real, sizeable phenomenon in retail generally | Established academic literature | High (general), Low (beer/India-specific) | Magnitude in this specific category and market |

## 7. Critical Assumptions — ranked by potential to change product direction

**1. Whether "value for money" is a real, natural cognitive frame, or one that would have to be taught.** Highest impact of anything in this model — if false, the founding mechanic of the entire project addresses a job nobody actually holds, and JTBD-3 collapses entirely rather than merely weakening.

**2. Where in the journey the real decision crystallizes — home, group chat, marketing exposure, or the shelf.** If the decision is mostly made before arriving, any product intervening only at or near the point of purchase is arriving after the moment that mattered has already passed.

**3. Whether legal-price fairness (JTBD-6) is a genuine individual concern, not just an institutional one.** Every piece of supporting evidence for this JTBD comes from officials or advocacy groups — if ordinary buyers don't personally track this, the "Beer Pricing Intelligence" direction rests on a real regulatory pattern that nobody individually cares enough about to act on.

**4. Whether beer is the right category at all, given spirits' revenue dominance.** Not fatal on its own — a smaller, well-served category can still be a real business — but it bounds how large this can ever become, and changes how the category choice should be defended, not just assumed.

**5. Whether narrowing and final-selection behavior in the Deliberate pathway is even influenceable by better information**, as opposed to being dominated by brand and packaging effects an app has no power to change. If the latter, better data might not change outcomes even where the underlying JTBD is real.

**6. Whether proxy-buying is common enough to warrant design attention.** Lower stakes than the above — this affects a feature's priority, not whether the business exists.

**7. Whether store-to-store price variation exists in any form beyond illegal overcharging.** Already substantially resolved as unlikely by prior research; retained here only because a future regulatory change (as happened in Delhi) could reopen it, and the model should not quietly assume that door stays permanently closed.
